I once heard the philosopher and spiritual teacher J. Krishnamurti describe why he was always so calm and composed. His answer was almost disappointingly simple: "My secret is I don't mind what happens."

That line stayed with me, and it keeps turning into a question I want to put to you: *What would you do differently if you didn't mind what happens?*

## Where the energy goes

Most of us spend a surprising amount of energy fighting things that have already happened. The reorg that just got announced. The meeting that went sideways. The feedback we didn't see coming. We argue with it in the car, replay it at night, and rehearse what we should have said. The fight feels like engagement, but it's mostly resistance, and resistance is expensive.

When you stop fighting what's already here, that energy comes back to you. You can spend it on seeing the situation clearly and responding to what's actually in front of you, instead of what you wish were there.

## Not minding isn't not caring

This idea is easy to misread. Not minding what happens is not indifference, and it's not giving up. You can care deeply about an outcome and still not mind what happens. Caring is about what you put in. Minding is about how tightly you grip what comes back. Leaders who don't mind still hold high standards, still push for what matters, and still feel disappointment. They just don't spend the first hour of every setback insisting it shouldn't have happened.

## The learner's advantage

Language teachers know this one well. The learners who become fluent fastest are rarely the ones who never make mistakes. They're the ones who don't mind making them. Fear of sounding wrong makes people speak less, risk less, and learn more slowly. The learners who let mistakes happen get more practice, more feedback, and more fluency.

Leadership works the same way. When you mind every awkward silence, every pushback, and every plan that doesn't land, you hold back. When you don't mind, you're free to listen instead of defend, to ask the real question, and to make the call without needing a guaranteed result. People around you feel that steadiness, and they often borrow it.

> **Try this week:** Pick one thing you're currently fighting: a decision you disagree with, a conversation you're dreading, or a result that let you down. Ask yourself, *"If I didn't mind that this happened, what would I do next?"* Then do that.
